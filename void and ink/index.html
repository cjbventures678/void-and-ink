— ANALYST NOTE: Subject is not what he appears. Proceed with caution. —

Cameron Belton grew up around engines. His father was an Air Force mechanic — a man who understood that machines, like people, will tell you exactly what's wrong with them if you're willing to listen close enough and get your hands dirty. That patience is in everything Cameron writes. The slow leak before the flood. The wrong sound at the right moment.

He is a husband. A father to a four-year-old boy he is building a world for. A man who goes to car shows with his dad on weekends and still feels something shift in his chest when a classic engine turns over clean. He plays video games the way other people read poetry — to feel something real inside something constructed. He has played Journey and understood that silence is a language. He has played Little Nightmares and understood that horror does not need to explain itself.

He loves Studio Ghibli for the same reason. My Neighbor Totoro has almost no conflict and is one of the most emotionally complete stories ever made. Cameron believes that the scariest thing is not the monster. It is the ordinary moment right before you realize something is wrong.

That is what Void & Ink is. Quiet dread. The horror that lives in the margins of ordinary life. Stories that do not chase you — they wait for you to look back and realize something has been following you for pages.

He writes because horror has always told the truth about the world when nothing else would. And because his son deserves stories with teeth — ones that teach you to look twice at the dark, not turn away from it.

The Man in 4B Has Not Moved in Eleven Days

PARANOIA DREAD By Cameron Belton

I started counting on a Tuesday.

Not intentionally. It was the kind of thing you notice by accident — the same light on at the same time, the same shadow behind the frosted glass of 4B's door when you walk past to your own apartment. The first few days I told myself I was imagining it. The fifth day I started leaving for work a little later just to see if anything had changed.

Nothing had.

His name is Gerald. I know this from the mailbox downstairs — the one with the handwritten label that someone covered in clear tape so it wouldn't smear. Gerald. Unit 4B. He has lived here longer than I have. His car, a brown 2004 Camry with a cracked left tail light, has not moved from spot 14 in the lower lot.

On day seven I knocked.

I don't know why I waited seven days. I tell myself it was courtesy. Respect for privacy. But I think the truth is that I already knew something was wrong and I wasn't ready to be the one who found it.

He answered.

He opened the door maybe four inches, chain still on, and looked at me from the gap. His eyes were fine. His voice was fine. He said he'd been under the weather. He thanked me for checking. He closed the door.

I stood in the hallway for a moment longer than I should have.

Something about his hands. The way they held the door. The knuckles. I've been trying to remember exactly what I saw and I can't place it but I've been thinking about his hands every night since and I don't think I'm going to stop.

Today is day eleven. His light is still on. His car is still in spot 14. I walked past his door this morning and I could hear something on the other side — low, rhythmic, like breathing or like something that had learned to sound like breathing.

I did not knock again.

She Kept Scratching Even After We Told Her to Stop

BODY HORROR By Cameron Belton

The itch started on a Monday. By Friday it had a shape.

Renee described it first as a patch — dry skin, she said, probably the weather. She had a habit of rubbing her left forearm when she was thinking and we all noticed she was doing it more. In meetings. At lunch. Once, in the middle of a sentence, she just stopped talking and scratched until someone said her name.

She apologized. She laughed it off. She wore long sleeves the week after that.

The shape she was drawing, without knowing it, was a letter. We only figured this out later, after she'd gone. Her desk neighbor Priya said she had watched Renee scratch the same spot every single day for two weeks — always the same movement, always the same location on her forearm — and she had thought it was a nervous habit. She hadn't said anything because what do you say.

What do you say.

The letter was a T. Or the beginning of something longer. The dermatologist said the scarring was consistent with repeated self-inflicted abrasion, which is a careful way of saying she had been writing on herself with her own fingernails without knowing it.

Renee said she never felt pain. Only relief. The itch would come and she would scratch and for a moment the pressure would ease and then it would return — slightly further along, slightly more specific, like something moving through her skin toward a destination.

She is on leave now. We send her cards.

Priya told me last week she has started scratching her own arm. Just lightly. Just to see.

She says it doesn't itch yet.

FILE NO. 0003

We Found the Dog Three Weeks Before It Went Missing

UNRESOLVED DREAD By Cameron Belton

Her name was Biscuit. Golden retriever, eight years old, a little gray around the muzzle. She had a red collar with her name and our phone number on a bone-shaped tag. She had been with us since my daughter was three.

We found her on a Tuesday evening, sitting at the end of the driveway like she'd been waiting. Same collar. Same tag. Same gray muzzle. My daughter ran to her before I could say a word.

We had not lost her. She had been inside all day. I had fed her that morning. She was in the house.

My wife thought it was a neighbor's dog. A coincidence. She held them side by side in the kitchen — the one that had been inside, the one from the driveway — and they looked at each other the way dogs don't look at each other. Not sniffing. Not circling. Just looking.

We called the number on the collar. Our own phone rang in my wife's pocket.

We kept the second one outside that night. I told myself we'd figure it out in the morning. I told my daughter the dog outside was a stray that just looked similar. She believed me the way kids believe things when they can tell you need them to.

In the morning the one outside was gone.

Three weeks later, so was ours.

Not lost. Not run away. I came downstairs and she was simply not there. Same bowl. Same leash on the hook. Same bone-shaped tag on the counter, next to her collar, which had been removed and folded neatly in a way no dog could manage.

We still have the bowl. My daughter still says goodnight to it.

I haven't told her about the tag.

— END OF FILE —
